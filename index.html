Garrett H. Kohno

PhD Candidate, Economics/Cornell University/Expected May 2027

I'm an economist who uses causal inference and experiments to study individual decision-making. My job market paper draws on over 20 million transactions to study how firms use promotional pricing to acquire customers, separating regulars who just shift purchases to the sale day from new customers who keep coming back. My other work shows that biased memory reinforces polarized beliefs, limiting what new information can do to change minds. I also compare simple and complex methods for measuring social norms, documenting key trade-offs for survey design. I'm on the 2026–27 job market.

Fields: Behavioral economics, experimental economics

Come for the Discount, Stay for the Station: Customer Capital and the Hidden Returns to Recurring Price Cuts

with Yi Han, David Huffman, and Yiming Liu

Should a store run the same discount every week? The cost shows up right away: a lower margin on every discounted sale, and at first most of the extra business is just regulars moving their purchases to the sale day. The payoff, if there is one, comes from new customers and builds slowly. We study this at a large fuel retailer where station managers set their own prices and about a third run a weekly discount day. Comparing around 600 launches with stations that never discount, we find that volume rises 12–30% and keeps growing for a year, as newly acquired customers keep coming back even after the discount ends. Yet a single station's books can't tell that gain from noise for most of a year, which may be why similar stations still price so differently.

Motivated Affective Polarization: Recall of relative partisan generosity

Sole authored

Efforts to reduce partisan animosity often work at first, then wear off. This paper asks whether memory is part of the reason. In online experiments, partisans see evidence on how generous their own party is compared with the other, then recall it either right away or a week later. Good news about their own side sticks. Bad news doesn't: partisans misremember it in their party's favor, and the distortion grows with time, so about half of the shift in their beliefs is gone a week later. These distorted memories feed polarized beliefs and discrimination against the other side, which helps explain why interventions built on new information fade.

Measuring Social Norm Variation Across Contexts: Replication and Comparison to Alternative Methods

with Huffman, Madiès, Vogrinec, Wang, and Yagnaraman · European Economic Review, 178

How do you measure what people think is socially appropriate? The standard method is a coordination game: people rate each action and are rewarded for matching how most others rated it. We compare it with simpler alternatives, asking people for their own view or for a guess about others' views, with and without pay. Every method agrees that taking money is less appropriate than giving it, even when the outcome is the same. But the more complex the method, the smaller the gap it detects and the more people it confuses: about half misunderstood the coordination game at first. Simply asking for people's own view was easiest to follow and best predicted behavior, though on controversial topics it may invite answers that sound good rather than honest ones.
