Garrett H. Kohno

PhD Candidate, Economics/Cornell University/Expected May 2027

I'm an economist who uses causal inference and experiments to study individual decision-making. My job market paper draws on over 20 million transactions to study how firms use promotional pricing to acquire customers, separating regulars who just shift purchases to the sale day from new customers who keep coming back. My other work shows that biased memory reinforces polarized beliefs, limiting what new information can do to change minds. I also compare simple and complex methods for measuring social norms, documenting key trade-offs for survey design. I'm on the 2026–27 job market.

Fields: Behavioral economics, experimental economics

Come for the Discount, Stay for the Station: Customer Capital and the Hidden Returns to Recurring Price Cuts

with Yi Han, David Huffman, and Yiming Liu

Should a retailer run a recurring discount? The cost, a lower margin on every discounted sale, is immediate, and part of the discount-day lift is intertemporal substitution by existing customers. The payoff from newly acquired customers builds slowly and needs a counterfactual the store never observes. We study this at a large fuel retailer that delegates pricing to station managers, where about a third of stations run a weekly discount day. Comparing around 600 launches with never-discounting stations in a difference-in-differences design, we find that volume at adopting stations rises 12–30% and keeps growing through the first year. Early on, the lift is mostly intertemporal substitution; over time, newly acquired customers carry the gain and keep buying after the discount ends. A single station's books cannot distinguish this gain from noise for most of a year, which may shed light on why similar stations continue to use different pricing strategies.

Motivated Affective Polarization: Recall of relative partisan generosity

Sole authored

Interventions can reduce affective polarization in the short term, but their effects often decay. This paper tests one explanation: individuals are motivated to sustain polarized beliefs about partisan groups even after receiving contrary information, and do so through memory. In online experiments, partisans observe signals of partisan generosity, with recall measured immediately or after one week. Partisans systematically misremember unfavorable information about their ingroup, and the distortion increases over time. These memory distortions have downstream consequences for polarized beliefs and intergroup discrimination, identifying motivated memory as a mechanism behind the persistence of affective polarization and a limit on interventions designed to reduce it.

Measuring Social Norm Variation Across Contexts: Replication and Comparison to Alternative Methods

with Huffman, Madiès, Vogrinec, Wang, and Yagnaraman · European Economic Review, 178

Studying how social norms vary across contexts requires reliable measurement. We revisit the Krupka and Weber (KW 2013) coordination-game method and compare it with a two-stage method that directly elicits first- or second-order beliefs about social appropriateness, with and without financial incentives. All methods replicate KW's main finding that taking money is less socially appropriate than giving money, holding outcomes fixed. However, the size of this gap varies across methods, from largest for first-order beliefs to smallest for KW, possibly because strategic uncertainty and complexity in KW attenuate its sensitivity to differences in norms. A comprehension check shows that about half of KW participants initially misunderstood the task, and first-order beliefs best predict actual behavior in simple dictator games. First-order beliefs may therefore be a simple alternative for capturing norm differences across contexts, though they may be more subject to social desirability bias where norms are controversial or pluralistic.
